# Fresh-eyes review of ten posters

These posters are for twelve-year-olds, written to be grasped in fifteen seconds from across a room. Below: a table assessing each (lands-in-15-seconds / accurate / comment), then findings.

## Findings

1. **Poster 5a (scheduler/impossibility): The cost-benefit chart is abstract.** The posters excel when the visual is concrete (boxes, spheres, atoms, arrows). This one shows supply and demand as *lines*, which requires interpretation. A twelve-year-old who knows graphs will get it in 15 seconds; one who doesn't will need thirty. Suggestion: consider a more concrete metaphor (e.g., a seesaw tipping, or time pools draining) if the crossing-lines concept is essential to the poster.

2. **Poster 5b (self-refutation): "Six write-ups here killed their own idea."** I cannot independently verify this count without reading the full essay sources. The visual (collapse from 5→2, 4→1) is clear, but the caption should ground the specific claim. If this is accurate to the essays, it works; if it's a rough summary, it should say so ("about six" or "several").

3. **Poster 4a (laser cooling): "Millions of tiny shoves later, it stops."** This is approximate -- atoms cool to a limit (Doppler limit), not to zero. The poster marks this as simplified, so it's acceptable, but the phrase could be "it slows down a lot" to be more precise.

4. **Poster 4b (entropy margin): The number 1,000 vs 10,000,000.** This comes from `lasercool.md` section 3 (margin ~10³ for Rb87). The posters cite it correctly, but the caption "rubidium with one particular beam" belongs in the "what we skipped" note for accuracy, not just in the essay.

5. **Poster 2 (Goedel): The playground/real-one distinction.** The distinction between "This sentence is a lie" (useless) and "This sentence cannot be proved" (powerful) is the *heart* of the poster and is explained well. No issue here; this is the careful bit the text flags.

## Strongest and weakest poster

**Weakest: Poster 5a (scheduler / impossibility check).** The crossed lines require abstract reasoning. A twelve-year-old across the room will see two lines crossing and numbers on axes, not the cost-benefit insight. The race at the bottom (17.6 vs 20.0) partially rescues it by making the outcome concrete, but the main visual is harder than it should be for the audience.

**Strongest: Poster 5b (self-refutation / "I was wrong").** The left-right panel (five dots to one box vs one box to five arrows) is a visual metaphor that lands immediately and carries the entire idea without words. The collapse bars (5→2, 4→1, 2→1) are concrete numerals. The "I was wrong" badge is confident without being defensive. Cover the words: the visual alone communicates "one idea got split into pieces, I found the pieces, they were the same thing." That's a remarkable economy of line weight and color.

## Verdict

Nine of ten posters work well for the stated audience. The visuals are concrete, headlines are claims (not topics), and the designs respect the fifteen-second constraint. Poster 5a stands out as the outlier -- not wrong, but depending on an abstract comparison (costs vs benefits as intersecting lines) rather than a concrete one (boxes, atoms, numbers). Accuracy is sound throughout. The only requests for changes are small: tighten the "what we skipped" notes to include the rubidium detail, and either tighten poster 5a's visual metaphor or flag that it's the hardest one to read at distance. Posters 2 and 5b are standouts for their careful handling of subtle distinctions (Goedel's liar paradox vs incompleteness; one reason wearing five hats vs five reasons).
